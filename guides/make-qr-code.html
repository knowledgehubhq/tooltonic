How to Make a QR Code in Seconds

By the ToolTonic editorial team · Published September 27, 2026

QR codes are everywhere now: restaurant menus, event tickets, product packaging, Wi-Fi login cards. Making one takes less than a minute, costs nothing, and requires no account. Here is how to do it right — including the differences between code types that most generators never explain.

What a QR code can actually hold

A QR code is just a machine-readable container for a short piece of text. The most common uses are:

  • A website link — the classic use. Scanning opens the URL.
  • Wi-Fi credentials — guests scan and join your network without typing a long password.
  • A contact card (vCard) — scanning adds your name, phone, and email to the phone's contacts.
  • Plain text — a short message, a coupon code, or instructions.
  • An email or SMS — pre-addressed, so scanning opens a composed message.

There is a hard limit on how much data fits: a few thousand characters at most, and the more data you pack in, the denser and harder to scan the code becomes. Short URLs scan far more reliably than long ones.

Static vs. dynamic: the choice that matters

Static QR codes bake the destination directly into the pattern. They work forever, cost nothing, and no company is involved — nobody can track scans or take the code offline. For almost every personal use, static is the right answer.

Dynamic QR codes point to a short redirect link controlled by the generator service, which then forwards to your real destination. The advantage is that you can change the destination later without reprinting the code, and you get scan statistics. The catch: they depend on the service staying in business, the free tier usually expires or limits scans, and the service can see every scan. Use dynamic codes only when you genuinely need to edit the link later — like a code printed on packaging for a campaign that might change.

Making one, step by step

  1. Pick a generator. For static codes with no account and no tracking, well-known options include the built-in generators in Chrome (right-click a page, "Create QR code") and standalone sites that offer free static codes without sign-up. Avoid any generator that forces you to create an account for a basic static code.
  2. Choose the content type (URL, Wi-Fi, contact, text) and enter your content. For links, use the shortest URL that works.
  3. Leave tracking options off unless you specifically want a dynamic code and understand the trade-off.
  4. Download the high-resolution version — PNG at the largest size offered, or SVG if available. A tiny low-res code looks fine on screen but prints blurry.
  5. Test it before you share or print. Scan it with two different phones (an iPhone and an Android if possible) using the default camera app, and confirm it opens exactly what you intended.

Design and printing tips

  • Keep contrast high. Dark code on a light background scans best. Fancy inverted or low-contrast designs look stylish and fail in real life.
  • Leave quiet space. The blank margin around a QR code is part of the code. Do not crop it or push text right up against the edges.
  • Size it for the distance. A code scanned from across a room needs to be much larger than one on a business card. As a rough rule, the code should be at least 2 cm (about an inch) wide for close-up scanning, and bigger the farther away the scanner will be.
  • Custom colors and logos are fine in moderation. Many generators let you add a small logo in the center or match brand colors. Keep the logo small (under 20 percent of the code area) and always re-test after styling, since heavy customization hurts readability.
  • Add a human-readable hint. A line of text under the code — "Scan for the menu" or "Scan to join Wi-Fi" — dramatically increases the number of people who actually scan it.

Privacy and safety notes

A QR code is public by nature: anyone who can see it can scan it. Never encode sensitive information like passwords (other than a guest Wi-Fi password, which is the intended use case), private links, or personal data you would not want a stranger to have.

On the scanning side, be a little cautious too: a QR code can point anywhere, including phishing pages. Before entering credentials or downloading anything from a scanned link, check that the URL looks legitimate. And if a generator asks for your email "to receive your free QR code," find a different generator — that is a mailing-list trap, not a technical requirement.

That is genuinely all there is to it. Pick static, keep it high-contrast, test before printing, and you will have a code that works on every phone, with no account and no cost.
